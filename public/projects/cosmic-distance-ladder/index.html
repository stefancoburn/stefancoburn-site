Rung 1 · Eratosthenes, Alexandria, c. 240 BC

The size of the Earth

At noon on the summer solstice the Sun shone straight down a well in Syene. At the same moment in Alexandria, a vertical stick cast a shadow. Sunlight arrives in parallel rays, so the shadow's angle is the angle between the two cities measured at Earth's centre.

The two marked angles are alternate angles cut by one sunbeam, so they are equal. Stick heights are exaggerated.

Rung 2 · Aristarchus and Hipparchus, 3rd–2nd century BC

The Moon, measured by Earth's shadow

During a lunar eclipse the Moon drifts through Earth's shadow at about one Moon-width per hour. Time the crossing and you learn how many Moons wide the shadow is. The shadow is a cone, and it narrows by exactly one Moon diameter on the way out, because the Sun and the Moon look the same size in our sky.

Side view, not to scale. The cone's edges converge at the Sun's angular size, about 0.5°.

Rung 3 · Aristarchus (c. 270 BC), Kepler (1619), Cassini and Richer (1672)

The Sun, and the Astronomical Unit

When the Moon is exactly half lit, the Sun–Moon–Earth angle is a right angle. Measure the angle between the Moon and the Sun in the sky, and the cosine gives the ratio of their distances. The method is sound, but near 90° it is ruthless.

The fix: one scale bar for Kepler's map

Kepler's third law (T² ∝ a³) maps the Solar System exactly, but only in AU. Mars's period of 1.881 years puts its orbit at 1.524 AU, and on the night of the 1672 opposition the map says Mars was 0.381 AU from Earth. One distance in kilometres scales the whole map. Cassini in Paris and Richer in Cayenne observed Mars at the same time and saw it shifted against the stars. Blink between their views and measure the shift.

Background stars are effectively infinitely far, so they don't move. Only Mars shifts. Tick marks are 5 arcseconds apart.

Rung 4 · Bessel (1838), Hipparcos (1989), Gaia (2013)

Stellar parallax

Earth's orbit gives a baseline of 2 AU. Over a year, a nearby star traces a tiny ellipse against far-off background stars. The parallax angle p is half the shift, and the distance in parsecs is simply 1/p in arcseconds.

Rung 5 · Hertzsprung and Russell (1910s), main-sequence fitting

Standard candles and the main sequence

Light spreads over a sphere, so flux falls as 1/d². Plot nearby stars, whose parallax distances we know, by colour and true brightness, and most land on a narrow band: the main sequence. A distant cluster shows the same band, dimmed by its distance. Slide it up until it fits. The size of the slide is the distance.

Cyan: nearby stars calibrated by parallax (absolute magnitude M). Amber: the cluster, plotted as apparent magnitude minus your distance modulus. Brighter is up.

Older clusters (M67, NGC 188) have lost their hot, bright stars and grown a branch of red giants. Fit the band, not the giants. Interstellar dust is ignored here; in real work it dims and reddens every cluster a little.

Carries up: some Cepheids live in clusters like these. Your cluster distances set the zero point of the Leavitt law in rung 6.

Rung 6 · Henrietta Swan Leavitt (1912), Edwin Hubble (1923)

Cepheid variables

Cepheids pulse like clockwork, and the brighter ones pulse more slowly. Measure the period, read the true brightness off the Leavitt law, compare with how bright the star looks, and out comes the distance. Rung 7 · Phillips (1993), Riess, Perlmutter and Schmidt (1998)

Type Ia supernovae

A white dwarf that nears 1.4 solar masses detonates and briefly shines like five billion Suns. They aren't perfectly identical, but brighter ones fade more slowly. Stretch a template light curve to fit, and the stretch tells you the true peak brightness.

Rung 8 · Slipher (1912), Hubble (1929)

Redshift and Hubble's law

Every element absorbs light at fixed wavelengths. In distant galaxies the same barcode appears, shifted red. Line up the lab wavelengths with each galaxy's dark lines to measure its redshift z, record it, and see velocity grow with distance.

Rung 9 · Friedmann, Lemaître, and the cosmic microwave background

The edge of the observable universe

The universe is 13.8 billion years old, yet we see farther than 13.8 billion light years, because space kept stretching while the light travelled. Add up each step of the light's journey, each stretched by all the expansion that came after, and you get the distance to the source today.

Distance today versus redshift. The curve flattens toward a limit: the particle horizon, the farthest a signal could have come since the Big Bang.

The Hubble tension

Real astronomers face the same problem at higher precision. The Cepheid-and-supernova ladder gives H₀ ≈ 73, while the cosmic microwave background, read through the standard model of cosmology, predicts about 67. The error bars don't overlap. Either a rung hides a subtle error, or the disagreement points to new physics.
